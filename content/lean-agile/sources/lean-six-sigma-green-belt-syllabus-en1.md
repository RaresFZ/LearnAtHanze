1. Foundations and the Toyota Production System

Lean Six Sigma combines two complementary traditions: Lean improves customer value and flow by
reducing non-value-adding work; Six Sigma reduces defects and undesirable variation through
disciplined measurement and analysis.

Lean versus Six Sigma

Lean asks where time, effort and resources are wasted across the value stream. Six Sigma asks how
reliably the process meets requirements and which variables explain its performance. Neither
approach is limited to manufacturing. A shorter process is not automatically a more capable
process; a statistically capable process may still be unnecessarily slow.

Toyota Production System (TPS)

TPS developed around respect for people and continuous improvement, supported by Just-in-Time
(produce what is needed when needed) and Jidoka (build quality into the process and stop to
address abnormalities). Standardized work, visual management, problem visibility and employee
problem-solving are supporting practices.

The 3Ms

Muda is waste (activity that consumes resources without adding value); Mura is unevenness or
irregular demand/workload; Muri is overburdening people or equipment. They reinforce one another:
unstable schedules (Mura) can cause overtime (Muri), errors and queues (Muda).

Kaizen and PDCA

Kaizen means continuous, incremental improvement involving the people doing the work. PDCA:
Plan a change and prediction; Do test on a small scale; Check/Study compare results with
prediction; Act adopt, adapt or abandon and standardize learning.

JIT, Jidoka, Heijunka and Andon

Just-in-Time coordinates production with demand; Jidoka stops or signals an abnormal condition;
Heijunka levels production volume and mix; Andon is a visual or audible signal requesting support.
These methods require stable processes and must not be confused with merely reducing inventory.

Worked application

A student-services team receives unpredictable batches of requests. Leveling intake review, making
exceptions visible and empowering staff to flag missing documents reduces queues without
requiring everyone to work faster.

Practice and reflection

Explain how Mura could cause both Muri and Muda in a university examination process. Identify a
practical Andon signal for IT support.

2. Lean principles, value and waste

The five Lean principles offer a sequence for improving an entire customer journey rather than
isolated tasks.

Five Lean principles

1 Value: define what the customer needs and is willing to recognize as valuable. 2 Value stream:
map all activities required to deliver it. 3 Flow: remove interruption, batching and rework. 4 Pull:
trigger work from actual downstream demand. 5 Perfection: continually improve quality, flow and
learning.

Value-added versus non-value-added

A strict value-added test asks whether an activity transforms the product or service, is done right first
time, and is valued by the customer. Some non-value-added work is currently necessary for legal or
risk reasons; classify this separately from removable waste.

Eight wastes: TIMWOODS

Transportation: unnecessary movement of materials/information between places; Inventory: excess
work in progress, stock or pending tickets; Motion: unnecessary movement by a person; Waiting: idle
time; Overproduction: producing too early or too much; Overprocessing: more work than needed;
Defects: errors and rework; Skills/unused talent: not using employee expertise and ideas.

Ninth sustainability lens

Environmental waste is sometimes added as a ninth waste: avoidable energy, water, emissions and
materials. It is not one of the universally standardized eight wastes; treat it as an explicit additional
sustainability lens and avoid double-counting.

Waste walk / Gemba walk

Visit the actual place where work occurs, observe without blame, record facts, distinguish
observation from interpretation, ask staff about obstacles, and discuss improvement ideas. Respect
privacy and safety. Gemba is learning at the real workplace, not an inspection to find fault.

Worked application

An IT ticket is copied between three teams (transportation), waits two days (waiting), is entered twice
(overprocessing), and is reopened because the initial fix failed (defects). An employee knows an
automation but is never consulted (unused talent).

Practice and reflection

Conduct a 20-minute waste walk: capture five observations, their type, evidence, impact and a
testable improvement idea. Distinguish motion from transportation.

3. People, belts and project governance

Belt names denote levels of training and responsibility; definitions and prerequisites vary among
certifying bodies.

Belts and roles

White Belt: awareness and basic terminology. Yellow Belt: participates in improvement activities and
data collection. Green Belt: leads bounded projects, commonly alongside normal duties. Black Belt:
leads complex analytical projects and coaches Green Belts. Master Black Belt: methodological
expert and deployment coach. Champion/Sponsor: senior leader who selects and supports
projects, removes barriers, secures resources and owns business alignment; this is a governance
role rather than a technical belt.

DMAIC and tollgates

Define clarifies the problem and customer. Measure establishes a trustworthy baseline. Analyze
validates causes. Improve tests and implements solutions. Control sustains gains. A tollgate is a
formal review at which a sponsor checks evidence and authorizes the next phase; it is not an
additional DMAIC phase.

Project Charter

A charter typically contains business case and Voice of the Business (VOB), problem statement,
measurable goal, scope and exclusions, process boundaries, stakeholders, team and roles, high-
level timeline, assumptions, risks and expected benefits. Avoid specifying a preferred solution in the
problem statement.

SMART goals and RACI

SMART goals are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. RACI clarifies
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed; normally only one accountable owner per
decision. Project governance should make data access and decision rights explicit.

Worked application

Weak charter: “Improve our app by implementing AI.” Stronger: “Reduce the median time to resolve
international-student onboarding questions from 72 to 36 hours by 30 June, for tickets handled by
the central helpdesk, without reducing first-contact resolution.”

Practice and reflection

Write a charter for ITIL exam results, IT service quality or an international-student app. Specify
sponsor, process start/end, baseline, goal, exclusions and DMAIC tollgate evidence.

4. Define: VOC, CTQ, charter and SIPOC

Define converts a vague complaint into an operationally meaningful improvement problem.

Voice of the Customer (VOC)

VOC is evidence of customers’ needs, expectations and experiences from interviews, surveys,
complaints, service logs and observation. Voice of the Business (VOB) covers strategic, financial,
regulatory and operational priorities. Distinguish what customers say from what the organization
assumes.

Critical to Quality (CTQ)

Translate broad needs into observable requirements: “fast support” → “90% of priority-2 tickets
receive an initial response within 4 business hours.” A CTQ needs an operational definition, target,
unit, measurement method and owner.

SIPOC

S = Suppliers; I = Inputs; P = Process (roughly 4–7 high-level steps); O = Outputs; C = Customers.
Work backward from customers and outputs if useful. SIPOC defines boundaries and interfaces; it is
not a detailed process map.

Stakeholders and scope

Identify customers, process owners, frontline employees, IT, compliance and sponsor. Clarify in/out
scope, upstream/downstream dependencies, data permissions and any foreseeable unintended
consequences.

Worked application

Coffee service SIPOC: suppliers = coffee supplier, milk supplier, bakery and guest; inputs = beans,
water, milk, biscuit and order; process = receive order → prepare drink → assemble order → serve →
collect payment; outputs = correctly served beverage and transaction; customers = guest and
business owner. The payment step may occur earlier in other variants: map actual practice.

Practice and reflection

Build a SIPOC for a university helpdesk. Define one VOC, two CTQs and a realistic process start and
end.

5. Process mapping, swimlanes and VSM

Mapping reveals the actual end-to-end process and handoffs that departmental dashboards often
conceal.

Flowcharts and swimlane diagrams

A flowchart shows steps, decisions, loops and start/end points. A swimlane diagram assigns
activities to actors, departments or systems. Cross-lane transfers reveal handoffs, ownership gaps
and repeated approvals. Map the current state before designing the future state.

Value Stream Mapping (VSM)

VSM shows material/information flows and often includes process boxes, cycle times, changeover
times, availability, inventories/queues and lead time. Draw current-state and future-state maps, use
measured rather than imagined data, and show customer demand and information triggers.

Process Cycle Efficiency (PCE)

PCE = value-added processing time / total lead time × 100%. Low PCE suggests that waiting,
queueing or handoffs dominate elapsed time; classification of value-added time must be consistent.

Mapping pitfalls

Do not mix minutes of staff effort with calendar days of customer lead time. Document parallel
paths, exceptions, rework loops, arrival patterns and measurement period. A future-state map is a
hypothesis until tested.

Worked application

An application requires 18 minutes of true value-added work but spends 3 days in queues. With 3
calendar days = 4,320 minutes, PCE = 18 / 4,320 × 100 = 0.42%. Clearly state whether nonworking
hours are included.

Practice and reflection

Draw a four-lane map (student, service desk, specialist, system) with at least two handoffs; create a
current-state VSM and propose one future-state change.

6. Measure: time, flow and capacity

Metrics must be defined at a common process boundary and with compatible units.

Takt time

Takt time = net available production/service time ÷ customer demand. It is the required rhythm to
match demand, not an observed processing time. Example: 420 available minutes/day ÷ 140
units/day = 3 minutes/unit.

Cycle time

Cycle time is the elapsed time between consecutive completed units at a process or the time
needed to complete one cycle, depending on convention. Define whether measuring station
processing time or output interval. If a station needs 4 minutes/unit and takt is 3 minutes/unit, one
such station cannot meet demand without redesign or parallel capacity.

Lead time and throughput time

Lead time is elapsed time from customer request to delivery, including processing and waiting.
Throughput time is often used similarly for time through a defined process; specify boundaries.
Processing/touch time is time actively worked on. Waiting can dominate total lead time.

Little’s Law

For a stable system using consistent averages, Work in Progress (WIP) = throughput rate × average
flow time. Thus flow time = WIP / throughput. Example: 80 open cases ÷ 20 cases/day = 4 days. This
is an average relationship, not a guarantee for individual cases.

Capacity, utilization and bottlenecks

Capacity is the maximum sustainable output under stated assumptions. Utilization = actual use /
available capacity. At high utilization with variable arrivals, queues can rise sharply. The bottleneck
is the limiting resource or step; improvements elsewhere may not increase end-to-end throughput.

First Time Right (FTR)

FTR = units completed correctly without rework on first attempt / units processed × 100%. Define
whether partial corrections, reopening and exceptions count as failures.

Worked application

A helpdesk has 7 hours of available staffed time and demand of 84 tickets per day: takt = 420/84 = 5
min/ticket. Average agent touch time is 8 minutes/ticket; a single agent cannot meet the pace,
although multiple agents may. Median ticket lead time is 2 days due to queueing.

Practice and reflection

Calculate takt, cycle-time gap and WIP implications for 360 available minutes, 120 requests/day, 4-
minute station cycle time and 60 open requests at 30 completions/day.

7. Measure: defects, yield, DPMO and sigma

Six Sigma requires a consistent definition of defect, unit and opportunity. These concepts are not
interchangeable.

## Page 8

Defect, defective unit and opportunity

A defect is one failure to meet a requirement. A defective unit has one or more defects. A defect
opportunity is a specified chance for one defect to occur per unit; its number should reflect
meaningful CTQs, not be inflated to make performance look better.

DPU, DPO and DPMO

DPU = total defects / total units. DPO = total defects / (units × opportunities per unit). DPMO = DPO ×
1,000,000. DPMO measures defects per million opportunities, not defective units per million.

Yield and rolled throughput yield

First-pass yield (FPY) = units passing a process without rework / units entering. For independent or
suitably defined sequential step yields, rolled throughput yield (RTY) = product of step FPYs. Do not
infer RTY from final inspection alone.

Sigma level

A conventional Six Sigma benchmark is often described as approximately 3.4 DPMO using a long-
term 1.5-sigma shift convention. The shift is a convention, not a universal physical law. Report actual
DPMO and assumptions rather than using sigma labels alone.

Measurement system and data quality

Use operational definitions, consistent defect opportunities, representative sampling, clear
timestamp conventions and repeatability checks. Inconsistent logging can create false
improvements.

Worked application

In 500 processed forms, 40 defects are found across 4 predefined opportunities per form. DPU =
40/500 = 0.08; DPO = 40/(500×4) = 0.02; DPMO = 20,000. If 35 forms contain defects, the defective-
unit rate is 35/500 = 7%, not 8%.

Practice and reflection

Compute DPMO for 1,200 units, 3 opportunities each and 54 defects. Explain why “54 defective
units” would not necessarily follow.

8. Analyze: root causes and problem solving

Analysis separates symptoms, correlations and plausible explanations from causes supported by
evidence.

Root Cause Analysis (RCA)

Define the observed failure precisely, map the mechanism, generate plausible causes, gather
discriminating evidence and verify whether changing the cause changes the outcome. A “root
cause” is useful when it is actionable and sufficiently explains recurrence.

5 Whys

Repeatedly ask why a condition occurred, using evidence for each link. Five is a mnemonic, not a
mandatory count. Complex problems may have several interacting causes; do not stop at a
convenient blame statement.

Ishikawa / fishbone

Place the problem at the fish head and organize possible causes on branches. Typical 6M categories
are Manpower/People, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement and Mother Nature/Environment;
adapt to services. A fishbone organizes hypotheses, it does not prove them.

Pareto analysis

Rank categories by frequency, cost, severity or another explicit measure and show cumulative
contribution. The 80/20 rule is a heuristic, not a guaranteed distribution. Prioritize on impact and
feasibility, not frequency alone.

A3 problem solving

An A3 is a concise one-page narrative of background, current condition, target, analysis,
countermeasures, implementation and follow-up. It encourages evidence, shared thinking and
coaching, rather than filling in a template for its own sake.

FMEA

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis anticipates failure modes, effects, causes and controls. Assess
severity, occurrence and detectability according to an agreed method; traditional RPN = S × O × D is
a prioritization aid with known limitations. High-severity risks deserve attention even when RPN is
modest.

Worked application

Problem: tickets reopen after closure. Why? Fix was incomplete. Why? Diagnostic checks were
skipped. Why? Agents followed inconsistent scripts. Why? Knowledge articles were outdated. Why?
No owner reviewed them after system changes. Verify with ticket samples and timestamps before
calling ownership the root cause.

Practice and reflection

Create a fishbone for late course-registration confirmations; write two competing causal
explanations and the evidence needed to distinguish them.

9. Analyze: data, variation and statistical thinking

Green Belts should distinguish common-cause variation from unusual events and avoid interpreting
every fluctuation as a trend.

Descriptive statistics

Mean summarizes arithmetic average, median is robust to skew, standard deviation measures
spread, percentiles describe tails. Show sample size, time window, missing values and distribution.
Lead times are frequently right-skewed, making median and P90 useful.

Common and special causes

Common causes arise from the current system; special causes are identifiable unusual influences.
Control charts use time order and statistically derived limits to help distinguish the two. Control
limits are not customer specification limits.

Control charts

Choose chart by data: I-MR for individual continuous measurements, X-bar/R for rational subgroups,
p-chart for proportion defective, u-chart for defects per unit with varying opportunity exposure.
Validate assumptions and adequate baseline before drawing conclusions.

Capability and stability

Cp = (USL − LSL)/(6σwithin); Cpk = min[(USL−mean)/(3σwithin), (mean−LSL)/(3σwithin)]. These
indices require meaningful specifications and suitable data/distribution assumptions; assess
stability first. A capable-looking unstable process is not reliably predictable.

Hypothesis testing and correlation

State a null and alternative hypothesis; choose a test consistent with data and design; report effect
size and uncertainty alongside p-values. Correlation does not establish causation. Prefer controlled
pilots, randomization where feasible, and before/after comparisons with contextual safeguards.

Worked application

Two teams average 12 and 10 hours to resolve tickets, but the second handles simpler tickets. The
difference alone does not establish superior performance. Stratify by priority and complexity, then
compare distributions and a controlled intervention.

Practice and reflection

Identify a suitable chart for weekly defective-form proportions, and explain why specification limits
must not be used as control limits.

10. Improve: flow, pull and production leveling

Improvement focuses on removing verified constraints and designing work that is easier to do
correctly.

Flow and one-piece flow

Continuous or one-piece flow moves a unit to the next step without unnecessary batching. It
reduces WIP and makes defects visible quickly, but requires suitable workload balance, reliable
steps and sensible exceptions.

Pull and Kanban

A pull system authorizes replenishment or work based on downstream consumption. Kanban
visualizes and limits work in progress, often using cards or digital workflow columns. Explicit WIP
limits help prevent overloading and expose blocked work.

Heijunka

Leveling reduces peaks in volume and mix where demand and operational constraints permit. It is
not a reason to ignore urgent priority work; define service classes and capacity buffers.

Line balancing and bottlenecks

Compare process step cycle times with takt; reduce or redistribute work at the constraint, protect it
from interruptions and avoid building excess upstream inventory. Recalculate end-to-end
performance after changes.

Poka Yoke and Jidoka

Poka Yoke means error-proofing or making errors difficult to create or easy to detect: keyed
connectors, required-format fields and barcode validation. Jidoka stops or alerts on abnormality and
triggers a response; detection without reaction is insufficient.

Worked application

A ticketing team limits “In Progress” to eight tickets, introduces mandatory ticket category
validation, and automatically flags missing diagnostic information before assignment. This
combines Kanban and Poka Yoke.

Practice and reflection

Propose a pull system for IT support and identify a condition where one-piece flow would not be
practical.

11. Improve: workplace, quality and changeover

Practical Lean tools should improve reliability, safety and accessibility, not simply make work look
tidy.

5S

Sort: remove unnecessary items. Set in Order: locate and label needed items. Shine: clean and
inspect. Standardize: agree visual standards and routines. Sustain: audit, coach and improve habits.
Some organizations add Safety as a sixth S.

Visual management and Andon

Make normal versus abnormal conditions immediately understandable with clear boards, queues,
status indicators and escalation rules. A dashboard that no one acts on is not effective visual
management.

Standardized Work

Document the best currently known safe method, sequence, timing, quality checkpoints and work-
in-process expectations. Employees should help design and revise the standard; standardization
enables Kaizen rather than freezing improvement.

SMED

Single-Minute Exchange of Die targets changeovers in single-digit minutes where feasible. Separate
internal activities (requiring shutdown) from external activities (possible while running); convert,
simplify, parallelize and standardize. “Single-minute” is an aspiration, not a guarantee for every
setting.

Daily huddles and stand-ups

Short daily meetings review safety, quality, delivery, workload, abnormalities, actions and
ownership. Escalate complex problems to separate problem-solving sessions rather than turning the
stand-up into a long status meeting.

Worked application

For a training room, Sort unused equipment, Set in Order cables and adapters, Shine/inspect
projectors, Standardize setup checklist and Sustain through a weekly ownership rotation.

12. Improve: maintenance, OEE and bottlenecks

Equipment performance can be improved only when losses and constraints are defined accurately.

TPM

Total Productive Maintenance combines proactive maintenance, operator involvement, planned
maintenance, quality maintenance and systematic loss reduction. Operators may perform safe
basic care; specialist maintenance and safety obligations remain necessary.

Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE)

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality. Availability = operating time / planned production time.
Performance = (ideal cycle time × total count) / operating time. Quality = good count / total count.
Use fractions, then multiply by 100 for percent.

Six big losses

Breakdowns, setup/adjustments, minor stops, reduced speed, startup rejects and production
rejects explain OEE losses. Classification rules should be stable and transparent.

Bottleneck analysis and theory of constraints

Identify the system constraint using throughput, queue and resource data; exploit and protect it,
subordinate other steps, elevate capacity where justified, then reassess. A locally busy resource is
not always the end-to-end constraint.

Worked application

Planned time 480 min, downtime 60 min, operating time 420 min; ideal cycle 2 min, total output 180,
good output 171. Availability = 420/480 = 87.5%; Performance = 360/420 = 85.7%; Quality = 171/180
= 95%; OEE = 71.25%.

Practice and reflection

Compute OEE if availability is 90%, performance 80% and quality 95%. Name one action for each
loss category.

13. Control: management, leadership and sustainability

A project is incomplete until the improved process is owned, monitored and able to respond to
deterioration.

Control plan

Specify CTQ/KPI, operational definition, target, data source, frequency, process owner, visual
display, reaction threshold, corrective action and escalation path. Train staff, update procedures
and audit adoption.

Lean leadership

Leaders establish direction, protect time for improvement, ask evidence-based questions at Gemba,
respect staff knowledge, remove barriers and avoid blame. Coaching and psychological safety
support honest problem reporting.

Hoshin Kanri

Policy deployment translates strategic priorities into a small set of breakthrough goals, annual
objectives, owners and review cycles. Catchball is a dialogue to align ambition, resources and
practical feasibility across levels.

Sustainable improvement

Measure not just speed and cost but service quality, staff workload, accessibility and environmental
effects. Reducing WIP should not shift hidden burden to suppliers or customers. Track benefits after
handover.

DMAIC versus PDCA

DMAIC provides a structured project path for an existing process problem; PDCA is a repeating
learning cycle for everyday and experimental improvement. They are complementary rather than
competing methods.

Worked application

A helpdesk controls response-time performance weekly using a p-chart for SLA failures, assigns the
service manager as owner, reviews causes after a statistically unusual signal, and checks for
unwanted increases in ticket reopenings.

Practice and reflection

Write a one-page control plan with an owner, three measures, thresholds and response actions;
explain how a daily stand-up supports the plan.

14. Integrated case study and solved calculations

Case: A university IT helpdesk wants to reduce long response times and repeated tickets while
keeping service quality stable.

Define

VOC: students need predictable and accurate support. CTQ1: first response within 4 business
hours. CTQ2: tickets resolved correctly without reopening. Charter: reduce SLA misses from 30% to
15% in one semester; scope standard student tickets; sponsor head of IT services.

Measure

In a month: 1,000 tickets, 300 miss the first-response SLA, 90 tickets reopen. First-response success
= 700/1,000 = 70%; FTR proxy (if reopening is the defined defect) = 910/1,000 = 91%. Do not call
reopening the only possible definition of FTR without agreement.

Analyze

Map lanes for student, intake, first-line, specialist and system. A Pareto may identify routing and
missing information as frequent causes. Validate by stratifying tickets and auditing a sample, not
merely by collecting opinions.

Improve

Pilot an intake form with error-proofing, routing rules, WIP limits and a daily blocked-ticket
escalation. Compare against baseline while controlling for ticket complexity and demand.

Control

Monitor weekly SLA miss proportion, reopen proportion and lead-time P90. Assign owners, publish
standards and agree reaction rules. Check workload and accessibility for unintended
consequences.

Worked application

Metric practice: demand = 100 requests/day, net available time = 400 min/day: takt = 4 min/request.
With 120 open requests and throughput of 40/day, average flow time ≈ 3 days if stable. With 30
defects across 600 requests and 2 defined opportunities/request, DPMO = 25,000.

Practice and reflection

Create a current-state VSM and SIPOC for the case. State two hypotheses and a pilot design;
calculate expected effect if SLA misses fall from 300 to 150 per 1,000 tickets (15 percentage points
absolute, 50% relative reduction).
